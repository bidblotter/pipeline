Do I need a permit for a deck in Raleigh, Cary & Wake County?

Short answer: yes, in most cases. If you are building a new deck, replacing one, enlarging one, or doing structural work on one, you need a building permit before work begins — in all three jurisdictions this site covers.

Short answer: yes, in most cases

All three jurisdictions say so in their own words:

  • Raleigh: “A building permit is required when installing a screened porch, deck, balcony, sunroom, or similar structure.”
  • Cary: “The North Carolina State Residential Code (NCRC) requires a building permit for all decks regardless of the size of the deck or the location of the deck on the lot.”
  • Wake County: “To build a new deck or porch you will need to submit” an application, site plan, and building plans through the Wake County Permit Portal.

Project cost alone does not decide it. Raleigh states this explicitly: work costing $40,000 or less can still require a permit when it involves load-bearing structures. A deck is a structure, so the “under $40,000” exemption that covers some non-structural interior work does not save a new deck.

What needs a permit vs. what doesn't

Needs a building permit:

  • Building a new deck (attached or freestanding)
  • Replacing an existing deck (a full rebuild, not just boards)
  • Enlarging a deck or changing its footprint
  • Structural repairs or alterations — footings, posts, girders, joists, ledger attachment, stairs, or railings as a structural system
  • A deck that supports a hot tub or spa, or a two-story deck (these need engineer-stamped plans in Wake County; Cary requires design by a North Carolina licensed design professional for spas/hot tubs on a wood deck system, cantilever floor systems, and point loads not supported directly to the foundation or footing)

Usually does NOT need a building permit (in-kind, non-structural work):

  • Replacing deck boards
  • Replacing pickets, railings, or stair treads
  • Other non-structural cosmetic repairs

These exemptions are stated in both the Town of Cary FAQ and Wake County's inspections FAQ. Note the limit: the moment the work touches structure — framing, footings, attachment to the house — it stops being a repair and starts being a permitted project. When in doubt, ask the jurisdiction before you start; that question is free and the wrong guess is not.

No small-deck exemption — read this before you assume otherwise

You may have heard that a small, low, detached deck (the kind of rule in some national code summaries: under a size limit, under 30 inches above grade, not attached to the house, not serving an exit door) doesn't need a permit. Do not rely on that here. Cary states the opposite explicitly — all decks, regardless of size or height. Wake County's pages list no size-based exemption for new decks, and Raleigh's pages state a building permit is required when installing a deck, with no small-deck carve-out. In this area, plan on a permit for any new deck.

Raleigh (inside Raleigh city limits)

Who handles it: The City of Raleigh Planning and Development Department, through its Permit and Development Portal. Register for an account, provide contractor details, or complete an Owner Exemption Affidavit if you are acting as your own contractor.

What to submit: A residential permit form, a certified survey, a site plan, and structural elevations.

Extra approvals that may apply:

  • Historic districts: A Certificate of Appropriateness from the Raleigh Historic Development Commission is required before you submit the permit application.
  • Septic systems: Properties with septic systems may need additional approvals.

Inspections: Raleigh states that when inspections are required, city inspectors check the work at key stages before the project is completed. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Permit expiration (from Raleigh's Unified Development Ordinance): A permit expires 6 months after issuance if the work has not commenced, and if work is discontinued for 12 months after commencement, the permit expires.

Cary (inside Cary town limits)

Who handles it: The Town of Cary Inspections & Permits Department (Cary Town Hall, 316 North Academy Street; 311 or (919) 469-4000). Apply online through the Town's electronic plan review portal; you will need an account for a first application. A homeowner may do their own work if the property will be their primary residence for at least 12 months after completion.

The rule, stated plainly: All decks, regardless of size or height, require a building permit under the North Carolina State Residential Code, with construction details governed by NCRC Appendix M.

What to submit:

  • Plot plan and proposed plot plan, to scale, showing the main dwelling outline, decks, porches, easements, and buffers, with distances to the property lines at the closest point. You may use an existing survey or a hand-drawn scaled site plan. A plot plan and as-built survey are required whenever a footprint changes — which includes decks — and an as-built survey is required if the proposed construction is within 5 feet of a setback, easement, or buffer.
  • Construction plans drawn to scale (1/4″ = 1′ recommended) showing a plan view, the designer's name and address, outside dimensions, footing size and location for each footing, girder size/type/location, floor joist size/type/spacing/direction of span, dimensions between supports, attachment to the dwelling (or a note that the deck is freestanding), cantilevers and their lengths, and treated floor decking per Appendix M AM107. Elevation drawings are optional but encouraged.
  • Details the code calls out: Bracing is required for decks attached to the house when the deck is more than 4 feet above grade, and bracing in each direction is required for all freestanding decks more than 30 inches above grade. Guardrails 36 inches in height are required when the deck is more than 30 inches above grade. A minimum of one electrical outlet is required within the perimeter of the deck and one accessible at grade level while standing on the ground (two outlets total) — show existing and proposed outlets on the plan so it is clear whether an electrical permit is also required.

Review timing: Decks under 500 square feet are eligible for Cary's SPOT review; the regular review cycle is approximately seven business days. Plan review for one- and two-family homes normally takes one to two weeks.

Inspections (typical for most Cary deck projects; your required list is determined by permitting staff before issuance and shown on your permit documents):

  1. Footing inspection — when footings are excavated, before concrete is placed (for existing footings, you may need to uncover them so the inspector can verify size).
  2. Foundation — deck/post placement on footers, and connections where required.
  3. Open-floor inspection — optional.
  4. Framing inspection — required if framing components will be obscured at final.
  5. Electrical inspection — when electrical work is complete, before anything is covered.
  6. Final inspection — when all work, including electrical, is complete, inspected, and approved.

Schedule inspections through Building Permits Online (24 hours a day, 7 days a week), in person at Town Hall (8:30 a.m.–5 p.m., Monday–Friday), or by calling 311 (or (919) 469-4000 from outside Cary).

Fees: Cary's deck document states fees are per the Town's current approved budget document — check Cary's fee schedule for the current amounts.

Wake County (unincorporated areas)

Who handles it — and how to tell: Wake County Inspections & Permits provides building inspections and permitting for all unincorporated areas of Wake County, and, through contractual agreements, inspection services for Knightdale, Rolesville, Wendell, and Zebulon. If you live inside one of those four towns, you apply through the town and Wake County provides the inspections. If you are unsure of your jurisdiction, look your property up on Wake County's iMaps site: if “WC” is listed as your planning jurisdiction, you are in Wake County's jurisdiction. Raleigh, Cary, Apex, Wake Forest, and other municipalities run their own departments and are not covered here.

What to submit (Wake County Permit Portal):

  1. A completed application using the Wake County Permit Portal.
  2. A site plan meeting the County's site plan requirements (entire lot drawn to scale, lot lines and dimensions, existing and proposed structures with dimensions and distances to property lines, driveways, wells/septic or sewer and water easements, water features and flood hazard areas, impervious surfaces itemized in square feet and as a percentage of net lot size, parcel ID, address, north arrow, and scale). A preliminary site plan is required before permits are issued; a final as-built site plan may be required before a Certificate of Occupancy.
  3. Building plans meeting the County's building plan requirements, drawn to the 2018 North Carolina Residential Code Appendix M: top view looking down (elevation plans optional but encouraged), outside dimensions, spans between support posts and footings, tallest height of the deck floor from finished grade, footing sizes (minimum 12 inches into the soil) and locations, post sizes, girder sizes and spans, floor joist direction/size/spacing/spans, ledger or joist hangers for attachment, guardrail details where required, bracing details, deck-to-house band attachment details, and cantilevers with spans.
  4. A certificate of workers' compensation insurance, or a completed affidavit of workers' compensation exemption, for contractors.
  5. A notarized affidavit owner-contractor form for projects costing $40,000 or more where an owner is listed as the general contractor.

Details the code calls out: Guardrails 36 inches tall are required for deck floors more than 30 inches above grade. Bracing details are required for freestanding decks and for decks attached to structures with a floor height of 4 feet or taller. Engineer-stamped plans are required for two-story decks and decks supporting hot tubs and spas.

Extra reviews that may apply:

  • Watershed management: If there are flood hazard areas or your proposed impervious area exceeds the allowable limit, a review is required; these reviews are $50 each, invoiced through the Permit Portal.
  • Septic/well: If the property is served by an onsite wastewater system, the new structure's location must be checked so it does not impact the system and maintains required setbacks from the wastewater system, its repair area, and any onsite wells. A field consultation fee and site visit by staff may be required. Contacts: Wastewater/Septic 919-856-7434, Groundwater/Well 919-856-7482.

Inspections: Schedule through the Wake County Permit Portal. To get a next-business-day inspection, submit the request before 3:00 p.m. on the previous business day. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Fees and licensing notes: A North Carolina licensed general contractor must perform work where the construction cost is $40,000 or more; a property owner may act as their own general contractor if they own the property and will personally occupy it for 12 months after completion. Deck permit fee amounts vary — check the County's current schedule (watershed reviews are $50 each).

What happens if you build without one

Raleigh's own residential permits page states the consequences plainly, and they are the right mental model for all three jurisdictions:

  • A citation from the city
  • Fines, increased permit fees, or possible court costs
  • Safety hazards in your home or on your property
  • Having to remove or redo work at the property owner's expense
  • Delays or complications when selling your home or property if unpermitted work is discovered
  • Insurance coverage issues related to unpermitted work

The cheapest version of this story is always the same: get the permit first, while the framing is still visible and the fix is still cheap.

HOA approval is separate from a permit

A city or county permit and your homeowners association's approval are two different things. A permit does not replace HOA architectural review, and HOA approval does not replace a permit — if your neighborhood has an HOA, expect to need both, and check your HOA's documents early, since HOA review runs on its own timeline.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for a small deck?

In Cary, yes — Cary requires a building permit for all decks regardless of size or height. Raleigh requires a building permit when installing a deck, and Wake County requires an application, site plan, and building plans for a new deck. None of the three jurisdictions states a small-deck exemption, so plan on a permit for any new deck.

Do I need a permit to replace deck boards?

No, not for in-kind replacement. Both Cary and Wake County list the replacement of deck boards — along with pickets, railings, and stair treads — as work that does not require a permit. The exemption ends where structural work begins: new footings, framing, or ledger attachment need a permit.

Do I need a permit to rebuild my whole deck?

Yes. A full rebuild is new construction in the eyes of all three jurisdictions, even if the replacement deck is the same size as the old one.

My deck is detached and low to the ground. Does that get me out of a permit?

Not in this area. Cary's rule covers all decks regardless of size, height, or location on the lot, and neither Raleigh nor Wake County publishes a detached-and-low exemption for decks. A freestanding deck still needs a permit; what changes is the detail (for example, bracing rules differ for freestanding decks), not whether a permit is required.

Can I do the work myself, or does a contractor have to pull the permit?

A homeowner may act as their own general contractor in Cary and in Wake County if they own the property and will personally occupy it for 12 months after completion (in Wake County, a notarized owner-contractor affidavit is required for projects costing $40,000 or more where the owner is the general contractor). In North Carolina, a licensed general contractor must perform work costing $40,000 or more. Raleigh's portal accepts either contractor details or an Owner Exemption Affidavit.

What inspections will my deck need?

Cary publishes a typical sequence: footing, foundation/post placement, framing (if it will be obscured), electrical (if any), and final — with the required list set by permitting staff before issuance. Raleigh and Wake County confirm inspections at key stages and scheduling through their portals; your permit documents will list the required inspections.

What if my property has a septic system or is in a historic district?

Expect extra approvals. Raleigh notes septic and historic-zone properties may need additional approvals, and historic homes need a Certificate of Appropriateness before the permit application is submitted. Wake County requires a location check against the septic system, its repair area, and any wells, with setbacks maintained.
