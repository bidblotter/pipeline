Do I need a permit for a pool in Raleigh, Cary & Wake County?

Short answer: yes. If you are installing a pool, spa, or hot tub that can hold more than the depth line — 18 inches of water in Cary, 24 inches in Raleigh and Wake County — you need a building permit before work begins — and in Raleigh and Wake County, an electrical permit too. That covers in-ground, above-ground, and on-ground pools.

Short answer: yes

All three jurisdictions say so in their own words:

  • Raleigh: “Permits and inspections are required for projects that can hold more than 24 inches of water. That includes: in-ground pools, above-ground pools, on-ground pools, spas, hot tubs.”
  • Cary: “Installing a swimming pool, hot tub or spa, including above ground pools; that contain 18” or more of water.” Cary's guide adds that it is “required by State law (G.S. 160A-417).”
  • Wake County: “All swimming pools, hot tubs and spas that can hold more than 24 inches of water require a building and electrical permit. A site plan showing the pool and its surrounding decking is required.”

The one number split: Cary triggers at 18 inches of water; Raleigh and Wake County trigger at 24 inches. That is the one place the three jurisdictions disagree on a number for the same project — an above-ground pool that holds between 18 and 24 inches of water needs a permit in Cary but, on the published wording, not in Raleigh or Wake County.

What needs a permit vs. what doesn't

Needs a building permit (plus an electrical permit in Raleigh and Wake County):

  • An in-ground pool
  • An above-ground pool
  • An on-ground pool
  • A spa or hot tub
  • Electrical work for the pool — pump, lighting, heater

Also required where it applies:

  • In Raleigh, a plumbing permit if the pool connects to the home water supply, for the backflow device
  • A barrier around the pool — required under the North Carolina Residential Code in Raleigh and Wake County, and in Cary the pool barrier fence must be permitted with the pool building permit

Usually does NOT need a building permit:

  • Nothing over the depth line is exempt in any jurisdiction.

Shallow or temporary water below the line is not stated exempt anywhere, either. Water at or under 24 inches in Raleigh and Wake County, or under 18 inches in Cary, has no published exemption on any official page — do not assume a small or inflatable pool is permit-free. Ask the jurisdiction before you fill it; that question is free and the wrong guess is not.

Raleigh (inside Raleigh city limits)

Who handles it: The City of Raleigh Planning and Development Department, through its Permit and Development Portal.

The rule: Building and electrical permits are required for any pool, spa, or hot tub able to hold more than 24 inches of water — in-ground, above-ground, on-ground, spas, and hot tubs alike.

Plumbing: A plumbing permit is also required if the pool connects to the home water supply, for the backflow device.

Where it can go: Pools may not sit over sewer or drainage easements or septic areas.

Barrier: A barrier is required around the pool under the North Carolina Residential Code.

At or under 24 inches: Raleigh's page does not state an exemption for pools able to hold 24 inches or less — it directs those questions to zoning staff (919-996-2492). Do not assume a shallow pool is exempt.

Inspections: Raleigh states that when inspections are required, city inspectors check the work at key stages before the project is completed. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Cary (inside Cary town limits)

Who handles it: The Town of Cary Inspections & Permits Department (Cary Town Hall, 316 North Academy Street; 311 or (919) 469-4000). Apply online through the Town's electronic plan review portal.

The rule, stated plainly: A building permit is required for installing a swimming pool, hot tub, or spa, including above-ground pools, that contain 18 inches or more of water. Cary's guide notes it is required by state law (G.S. 160A-417).

Setbacks: The pool and its decking and equipment must stay 5 feet from side and rear property lines.

Barrier: The pool barrier fence must be permitted with the pool building permit. A portable spa or hot tub with an ASTM-compliant safety cover is exempt from the barrier rule (LDO 5.2.2) — that is an exemption from the barrier, not from the permit.

Fees: Check Cary's fee schedule for the current amounts.

Wake County (unincorporated areas)

Who handles it — and how to tell: Wake County Inspections & Permits provides building inspections and permitting for all unincorporated areas of Wake County, and, through contractual agreements, inspection services for Knightdale, Rolesville, Wendell, and Zebulon. If you live inside one of those four towns, you apply through the town and Wake County provides the inspections. If you are unsure of your jurisdiction, look your property up on Wake County's iMaps site: if “WC” is listed as your planning jurisdiction, you are in Wake County's jurisdiction. Raleigh, Cary, Apex, Wake Forest, and other municipalities run their own departments and are not covered here.

What to submit (Wake County Permit Portal):

  1. A completed application using the Wake County Permit Portal.
  2. A site plan showing the pool and its surrounding decking.

Barrier: All pools, spas, and hot tubs must be enclosed by a barrier under NCRC Appendix V.

Extra reviews that may apply:

  • Septic/well: If the property is served by an onsite wastewater system, a septic and well setback check is required.
  • Watershed management: If there are flood hazard areas or your proposed impervious area exceeds the allowable limit, a watershed review may apply — check the County's current fee schedule.

Inspections: Schedule through the Wake County Permit Portal. To get a next-business-day inspection, submit the request before 3:00 p.m. on the previous business day. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Fees and licensing notes: A North Carolina licensed general contractor must perform work where the construction cost is $40,000 or more; a property owner may act as their own general contractor if they own the property and will personally occupy it for 12 months after completion. Check the County's current fee schedule for permit amounts.

What happens if you build without one

Raleigh's own residential permits page states the consequences plainly, and they are the right mental model for all three jurisdictions:

  • A citation from the city
  • Fines, increased permit fees, or possible court costs
  • Safety hazards in your home or on your property
  • Having to remove or redo work at the property owner's expense
  • Delays or complications when selling your home or property if unpermitted work is discovered
  • Insurance coverage issues related to unpermitted work

The cheapest version of this story is always the same: get the permit first, while the work is still visible and the fix is still cheap.

HOA approval is separate from a permit

A city or county permit and your homeowners association's approval are two different things. A permit does not replace HOA architectural review, and HOA approval does not replace a permit — if your neighborhood has an HOA, expect to need both, and check your HOA's documents early, since HOA review runs on its own timeline.

Verify before you build

Permit rules change, and the details that matter — setbacks, floodplain and watershed reviews, septic clearances, historic-district approvals — are specific to your lot. Before you build, confirm your jurisdiction (Cary, Raleigh, or Wake County — see the iMaps check above for unincorporated Wake) and confirm the current requirements with that jurisdiction's office. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for an above-ground pool?

Yes, in all three jurisdictions. Cary requires a building permit for any above-ground pool that contains 18 inches or more of water. Raleigh and Wake County require building and electrical permits for any pool able to hold more than 24 inches of water, and both name above-ground pools explicitly.

Why does Cary say 18 inches and Raleigh and Wake County say 24?

Cary's threshold is stricter than the other two — the one place the three jurisdictions disagree on a number for the same project. An above-ground pool that holds between 18 and 24 inches of water needs a permit in Cary but, on the published wording, not in Raleigh or Wake County.

Do hot tubs and spas need a permit?

Yes. All three jurisdictions treat spas and hot tubs the same as pools once they can hold water past the depth line — 18 inches or more in Cary, more than 24 inches in Raleigh and Wake County. In Cary, a portable spa or hot tub with an ASTM-compliant safety cover is exempt from the barrier rule, not from the permit.

What about a small or temporary pool — a kiddie pool or an inflatable?

No exemption is published anywhere for shallow or temporary water. Water below the depth line (24 inches or less in Raleigh and Wake County, under 18 inches in Cary) is not stated exempt on any official page, so do not assume a small pool is permit-free — ask the jurisdiction first. In Raleigh, questions about pools at or under 24 inches go to zoning staff at 919-996-2492.

Does the pool need a fence or barrier?

Yes, a barrier is required — Raleigh requires one under the North Carolina Residential Code, and Wake County requires all pools, spas, and hot tubs to be enclosed by a barrier under NCRC Appendix V. In Cary, the pool barrier fence must be permitted with the pool building permit.

Can I put a pool over a septic system or an easement?

No. Raleigh states pools may not sit over sewer or drainage easements or septic areas. Wake County requires a septic and well setback check where the property is served by an onsite wastewater system. In Cary, the pool and its decking and equipment must stay 5 feet from side and rear property lines.

Do I need separate electrical and plumbing permits?

Raleigh and Wake County require a building permit and an electrical permit for the pool; Cary requires a building permit. In Raleigh, a plumbing permit is also required if the pool connects to the home water supply, for the backflow device.
