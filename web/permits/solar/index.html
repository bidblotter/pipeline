Do I need a permit for solar panels in Raleigh, Cary & Wake County?

Short answer: yes, at any size, in all three jurisdictions. There is no small-system exemption anywhere. The permit itself is the easy part for most rooftop systems — Raleigh offers same-day online permitting for most of them — and the thing that changes by address is whether the system is roof-mounted or ground-mounted: the two follow different records.

Short answer: yes, at any size

All three jurisdictions say so in their own words:

  • Raleigh: “Most residential one and two-family rooftop solar PV systems are eligible for same-day online permitting through the Permit Portal.” And: “A Zoning Permit is not required for roof mounted solar systems that do not exceed the height allowed within the UDO.”
  • Cary: “Solar or photovoltaic equipment, includes re-installing equipment when a roof is replaced” needs a permit. Cary's solar guide adds: “Roof-mounted systems require Building Permits.”
  • Wake County: “Apply on the Permit Portal for the permits you'll need.” The County's solar checklist sets the record by mounting type: “Residential existing / Roof-mounted → Residential alteration” and “Ground-mounted → Residential non-building structures.”

The split that matters — roof vs. ground: roof-mounted systems need no zoning permit in Raleigh and Cary and file as a Residential alteration in Wake County. Ground-mounted systems need a site plan in Raleigh and file as Residential non-building structures in Wake County — and the pads and access drive count toward impervious surface.

What needs a permit vs. what doesn't

Needs a permit:

  • Any residential solar PV system, of any size, in all three jurisdictions
  • Re-installing solar equipment when a roof is replaced (Cary names this explicitly)
  • Ground-mounted systems — with a site plan in Raleigh and a non-building-structures record in Wake County

Usually does NOT need a permit:

  • A zoning permit for a roof-mounted system — in Raleigh, when the system does not exceed the height allowed in the zoning district, and in Cary, where no zoning permit is required for residential solar at all. The building and electrical permits are still required.

There is no size exemption anywhere: a two-panel system needs a permit just as a full-roof array does. What changes with size and mounting is the record type and the review, not whether a permit is required.

Raleigh (inside Raleigh city limits)

Who handles it: The City of Raleigh Planning and Development Department, through its Permit and Development Portal.

The rule: A residential permit is required for a rooftop solar PV system on a one- or two-family home. The electrical permit is applied for through the residential permit, by listing the electrical subcontractor's information.

Rooftop — the fast path: Most residential one- and two-family rooftop systems are eligible for same-day online permitting through the Permit Portal. No site plan is required for a rooftop project, and no zoning permit is required for a roof-mounted system that does not exceed the height allowed in the zoning district. Rooftop systems are allowed in all residential zoning districts and may exceed the district's maximum building height by up to 12 feet.

Ground-mounted: A site plan is required. Apply with the Residential Permit Application, selecting “Other” as the work type, by email.

What to submit: The Residential Permit Application; plans including the PV array, inverters, combiners, controllers, mounting system, and wiring schematic; specification sheets and the manufacturer's installation instructions for every component; and a signed and sealed document from a design professional who performed a solar onsite inspection, stating that the structural installation was designed and inspected, that the equipment will not harm the building's design, and that the work complies with the North Carolina State Residential Code.

Timing: You must begin work within six months of permit issuance — if work has not begun after six months, the permit expires. If work begins and is then discontinued for 12 months, the permit expires immediately.

Separate from the permit: Utility interconnection with Duke Energy Progress is a separate process from the city permit.

Inspections: Once issued, schedule the inspection through the Permit Portal — typically a final inspection. A Certificate of Compliance follows the design professional's structural documentation and the Building Official's approval.

Cary (inside Cary town limits)

Who handles it: The Town of Cary Inspections & Permits Department (Cary Town Hall, 316 North Academy Street; 311 or (919) 469-4000). Apply online through the Town's electronic plan review portal.

The rule, stated plainly: A building permit is required for residential solar installations, and no zoning permit is required. Cary's FAQ lists solar and photovoltaic equipment on its permit-required list — including re-installing equipment when a roof is replaced — and its solar guide states that roof-mounted systems require building permits.

Inspections: PV installations are inspected for compliance with the applicable North Carolina codes and the manufacturer's instructions. Inspections are required for electrical work and for building work on rooftop or structure-mounted installations; plumbing and mechanical inspections apply where relevant. The required inspections are set by plan review staff and listed on the permit documents at issuance.

Third-party inspections: Where a licensed architect or engineer performs a component or element inspection, the procedure follows the form approved by the North Carolina Building Code Council under state law (NCGS 160D-1106).

Fees: Check Cary's fee schedule for the current amounts. Cary is a U.S. Department of Energy SolSmart Gold community.

Wake County (unincorporated areas)

Who handles it — and how to tell: Wake County Inspections & Permits provides building inspections and permitting for all unincorporated areas of Wake County, and, through contractual agreements, inspection services for Knightdale, Rolesville, Wendell, and Zebulon. If you live inside one of those four towns, you apply through the town and Wake County provides the inspections. If you are unsure of your jurisdiction, look your property up on Wake County's iMaps site: if “WC” is listed as your planning jurisdiction, you are in Wake County's jurisdiction. Raleigh, Cary, Apex, Wake Forest, and other municipalities run their own departments and are not covered here.

The rule: A permit is required for a residential solar system at any size. Wake County's solar eligibility page points to the County's solar checklist, which sets the permit record by mounting type.

Which record:

  • Roof-mounted, existing home: Residential alteration
  • Ground-mounted: Residential non-building structures

How to apply: Apply on the Wake County Permit Portal for the permits you need. The County's solar pathway starts with a sketch of the property showing the house, all structures, and the proposed system, then a conversation with a licensed installer or County staff before the submittal package is prepared.

Extra reviews that may apply: For a ground-mounted system, the pads and access drive count toward impervious surface, so a watershed or floodplain review may apply. Other planning and zoning rules may apply since every project is site-specific — the County's own guidance says the best source for a complete list of requirements is County staff (919-856-6222).

Inspections: Schedule through the Wake County Permit Portal. To get a next-business-day inspection, submit the request before 3:00 p.m. on the previous business day. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

What happens if you build without one

Raleigh's own residential permits page states the consequences plainly, and they are the right mental model for all three jurisdictions:

  • A citation from the city
  • Fines, increased permit fees, or possible court costs
  • Safety hazards in your home or on your property
  • Having to remove or redo work at the property owner's expense
  • Delays or complications when selling your home or property if unpermitted work is discovered
  • Insurance coverage issues related to unpermitted work

The cheapest version of this story is always the same: get the permit first, while the work is still visible and the fix is still cheap.

HOA approval is separate from a permit

A city or county permit and your homeowners association's approval are two different things. A permit does not replace HOA architectural review, and HOA approval does not replace a permit — if your neighborhood has an HOA, expect to need both, and check your HOA's documents early, since HOA review runs on its own timeline. For solar specifically: an HOA cannot prohibit solar panels outright under North Carolina law (G.S. 22B-20), but it may regulate where the panels go and how they are screened.

Verify before you build

Permit rules change, and the details that matter — setbacks, floodplain and watershed reviews, septic clearances, historic-district approvals — are specific to your lot. Before you build, confirm your jurisdiction (Cary, Raleigh, or Wake County — see the iMaps check above for unincorporated Wake) and confirm the current requirements with that jurisdiction's office. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for solar panels on my roof?

Yes, at any size, in all three jurisdictions. A rooftop system is the simplest path: no zoning permit is required in Raleigh or Cary, no site plan is required in Raleigh, and most rooftop systems in Raleigh qualify for same-day online permitting. In Wake County a roof-mounted system files as a Residential alteration.

What is different about ground-mounted solar?

Everything about the paperwork. In Raleigh a ground-mounted system needs a site plan and applies through the Residential Permit Application with “Other” as the work type, by email. In Wake County it files as Residential non-building structures rather than a Residential alteration, and the pads and access drive count toward impervious surface, so a watershed or floodplain review may apply.

Do I need a zoning permit for rooftop solar?

No, in Raleigh and Cary — a zoning permit is not required for roof-mounted systems that do not exceed the height allowed in the zoning district. In Raleigh, rooftop systems are allowed in all residential zoning districts and may exceed the district's maximum building height by up to 12 feet. In Wake County the system files as a Residential alteration rather than a zoning case.

How fast is the solar permit in Raleigh?

Most residential one- and two-family rooftop solar PV systems are eligible for same-day online permitting through the Permit Portal. A small percentage of projects get flagged in the portal for further review, in which case staff review the application for completeness and route it for stormwater and multi-trade plan review.

Do I need an engineer for a solar permit?

In Raleigh, yes — the application must include a signed and sealed document from a design professional who performed a solar onsite inspection, stating that the structural installation was designed and inspected, that the equipment will not harm the building's design, and that the work complies with the North Carolina State Residential Code. In Cary, third-party design professional inspections follow the procedure in state law (NCGS 160D-1106).

Does my HOA have to approve solar panels?

HOA approval is separate from the permit — you may need both. But an HOA cannot prohibit solar panels outright under North Carolina law (G.S. 22B-20). It may regulate where the panels go and how they are screened, so check your HOA's documents early.

Do I need a separate approval from Duke Energy?

Yes. Utility interconnection with Duke Energy Progress is separate from the city or county permit — the permit covers the installation, and interconnection is its own process with the utility. Plan for both timelines, not just the permit.
