Do I need a permit for a home addition in Raleigh, Cary & Wake County?

Short answer: yes. If you are adding new space to your home — expanding the footprint, adding a second floor, enclosing a garage, or building a sunroom or dormer — you need a building permit before work begins — in all three jurisdictions this site covers.

Short answer: yes

All three jurisdictions say so in their own words:

  • Raleigh: “Additions include but are not limited to: Adding new space to an existing structure; Adding to the footprint; Adding a second floor; Enclosing a garage; Attached mother-in-law suites; Sunrooms; Dormers.”
  • Cary: “Interior and/or exterior wall demolition or addition” is on the permit-required list.
  • Wake County: “Additions that increase the footprint of the structure require a full site plan, including impervious surface calculations” — as part of the building permit submission.

Project cost alone does not decide it. Raleigh states this explicitly: work costing $40,000 or less can still require a permit when it involves load-bearing structures. An addition is structural work, so the “under $40,000” exemption that covers some non-structural interior work does not save a new addition.

What needs a permit vs. what doesn't

Needs a building permit:

  • Adding new space to an existing structure
  • Adding to the footprint of the house
  • Adding a second floor
  • Enclosing a garage
  • Attached mother-in-law suites
  • Sunrooms and dormers

Usually does NOT need a building permit:

  • Nothing, for a true addition — in any of the three jurisdictions. There is no small-addition exemption on any of their pages.

Know the line: converting unfinished space inside the existing footprint is an alteration, not an addition. It still usually needs a permit if it involves structural work or changes to plumbing, electrical, or mechanical systems — but it goes through the alteration process, not the addition process. When in doubt, ask the jurisdiction before you start; that question is free and the wrong guess is not.

No small-addition exemption — and SPOT review is not an exemption

You may have heard that a small addition can skip the permit process. Do not rely on that here. None of the three jurisdictions publishes a size-based exemption for additions. Cary does offer its SPOT expedited review for additions under 500 square feet — but that is faster review, not no permit: the building permit is still required. In this area, plan on a permit for any addition, whatever its size.

Raleigh (inside Raleigh city limits)

Who handles it: The City of Raleigh Planning and Development Department, through its Permit and Development Portal. Register for an account, provide contractor details, or complete an Owner Exemption Affidavit if you are acting as your own contractor.

What to submit: An application, a plan set, a site plan, elevations, and structural plans.

Extra triggers on Raleigh's Accessory Structures and Additions page:

  • 800 or more square feet of new impervious surface triggers a Lot Grading Plan.
  • A height over 15 feet triggers a Residential Permit Data Form.

Extra approvals that may apply:

  • Historic districts: A Certificate of Appropriateness from the Raleigh Historic Development Commission is required before you submit the permit application.
  • Septic systems: Properties with septic systems may need additional approvals, including Wake County Environmental Services approval before applying.

Inspections: Raleigh states that when inspections are required, city inspectors check the work at key stages before the project is completed. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Cary (inside Cary town limits)

Who handles it: The Town of Cary Inspections & Permits Department (Cary Town Hall, 316 North Academy Street; 311 or (919) 469-4000). Apply online through the Town's electronic plan review portal; you will need an account for a first application. A homeowner may do their own work if the property will be their primary residence for at least 12 months after completion.

The rule, stated plainly: “Interior and/or exterior wall demolition or addition” is on Cary's permit-required list. There is no size-based exemption.

What to submit: A footprint change requires a plot plan and as-built survey. Apply through the Town's electronic plan review portal.

Review timing: Additions under 500 square feet are eligible for Cary's SPOT expedited review.

Inspections: Your required list is determined by permitting staff before issuance and shown on your permit documents.

Fees: Cary's fees are per the Town's current approved budget document — check Cary's fee schedule for the current amounts.

Wake County (unincorporated areas)

Who handles it — and how to tell: Wake County Inspections & Permits provides building inspections and permitting for all unincorporated areas of Wake County, and, through contractual agreements, inspection services for Knightdale, Rolesville, Wendell, and Zebulon. If you live inside one of those four towns, you apply through the town and Wake County provides the inspections. If you are unsure of your jurisdiction, look your property up on Wake County's iMaps site: if “WC” is listed as your planning jurisdiction, you are in Wake County's jurisdiction. Raleigh, Cary, Apex, Wake Forest, and other municipalities run their own departments and are not covered here.

What to submit (Wake County Permit Portal):

  1. A completed application using the Wake County Permit Portal.
  2. A site plan meeting the County's site plan requirements. Additions that increase the footprint require a full site plan, including impervious surface calculations. For an addition or alteration within the existing footprint, a site plan is required only if the number of bedrooms increases and the property is served by an onsite wastewater system.
  3. Building plans meeting the County's building plan requirements.
  4. A certificate of workers' compensation insurance, or a completed affidavit of workers' compensation exemption, for contractors.
  5. A notarized affidavit owner-contractor form for projects costing $40,000 or more where an owner is listed as the general contractor.

Extra reviews that may apply:

  • Septic/well: If the property is served by an onsite wastewater system, the new structure's location must be checked so it does not impact the system and maintains required setbacks from the wastewater system, its repair area, and any onsite wells. If an addition increases the bedroom count on a septic-served lot, the septic system must be reviewed for capacity.

Inspections: Schedule through the Wake County Permit Portal. To get a next-business-day inspection, submit the request before 3:00 p.m. on the previous business day. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Fees and licensing notes: A North Carolina licensed general contractor must perform work where the construction cost is $40,000 or more; a property owner may act as their own general contractor if they own the property and will personally occupy it for 12 months after completion. Permit fee amounts vary — check the County's current schedule.

What happens if you build without one

Raleigh's own residential permits page states the consequences plainly, and they are the right mental model for all three jurisdictions:

  • A citation from the city
  • Fines, increased permit fees, or possible court costs
  • Safety hazards in your home or on your property
  • Having to remove or redo work at the property owner's expense
  • Delays or complications when selling your home or property if unpermitted work is discovered
  • Insurance coverage issues related to unpermitted work

The cheapest version of this story is always the same: get the permit first, while the framing is still visible and the fix is still cheap.

HOA approval is separate from a permit

A city or county permit and your homeowners association's approval are two different things. A permit does not replace HOA architectural review, and HOA approval does not replace a permit — if your neighborhood has an HOA, expect to need both, and check your HOA's documents early, since HOA review runs on its own timeline.

Verify before you build

Permit rules change, and the details that matter — setbacks, floodplain and watershed reviews, septic clearances, historic-district approvals — are specific to your lot. Before you build, confirm your jurisdiction (Cary, Raleigh, or Wake County — see the iMaps check above for unincorporated Wake) and confirm the current requirements with that jurisdiction's office. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for a small addition?

Yes. Raleigh, Cary, and Wake County all require a building permit for an addition of any size — none of the three publishes a small-addition exemption. In Cary, additions under 500 square feet are eligible for the town's SPOT expedited review, but that is faster review, not no permit.

Do I need a permit to add a second floor?

Yes. Raleigh lists adding a second floor explicitly among the projects that are additions, and all three jurisdictions treat a second floor as an addition requiring a building permit with plans.

Do I need a permit to enclose my garage?

Yes. Raleigh lists enclosing a garage explicitly as an addition. Enclosing a garage changes the structure and its use, so it needs a building permit with plans in all three jurisdictions.

I'm finishing space inside my existing footprint — is that an addition?

No — that is an alteration, not an addition. An addition increases the footprint or adds new space, such as a second floor or a garage enclosure. Finishing unfinished space inside the existing footprint is still usually a permit job if it involves structural work or changes to plumbing, electrical, or mechanical systems, but it goes through the alteration process rather than the addition process.

Can I do the work myself, or does a contractor have to pull the permit?

A homeowner may act as their own general contractor in Cary and in Wake County if they own the property and will personally occupy it for 12 months after completion (in Wake County, a notarized owner-contractor affidavit is required for projects costing $40,000 or more where the owner is the general contractor). In North Carolina, a licensed general contractor must perform work costing $40,000 or more. Raleigh's portal accepts either contractor details or an Owner Exemption Affidavit.

What extra paperwork might my addition trigger in Raleigh?

Two thresholds on Raleigh's Accessory Structures and Additions page: 800 or more square feet of new impervious surface triggers a Lot Grading Plan, and a height over 15 feet triggers a Residential Permit Data Form. Both are in addition to the standard submission package of application, plan set, site plan, elevations, and structural plans.

What if my property has a septic system or is in a historic district?

Expect extra approvals. Raleigh notes septic and historic-zone properties may need additional approvals, and historic homes need a Certificate of Appropriateness before the permit application is submitted. Wake County requires a location check against the septic system, its repair area, and any wells, with setbacks maintained — and if an addition increases the bedroom count on a septic-served lot, the septic system must be reviewed for capacity.
