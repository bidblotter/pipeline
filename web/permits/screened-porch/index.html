Do I need a permit for a screened porch in Raleigh, Cary & Wake County?

Short answer: yes. If you are building a new screened porch, screening in an open porch, or converting a porch into a roofed structure, you need a building permit before work begins — in all three jurisdictions this site covers.

Short answer: yes

All three jurisdictions say so in their own words:

  • Raleigh: “A building permit is required when installing a screened porch, deck, balcony, sunroom, or similar structure.”
  • Cary: “Screened or open porch” is on the permit-required list in the Town's Residential Permits & Inspections FAQ.
  • Wake County: “To build a new deck or porch you will need to submit” an application, site plan, and building plans through the Wake County Permit Portal.

There is no small-porch exemption in any of the three jurisdictions. Raleigh's rule is the same sentence as the deck rule — the same words cover a screened porch and a deck alike. If you are building a deck instead, see our deck permit guide. Note the contrast with Cary's deck rule, which is “all decks regardless of size or height”: the porch rule is a plain list entry with no size language — same outcome (a permit is required), different wording.

What needs a permit vs. what doesn't

Needs a building permit:

  • Building a new screened porch
  • Screening in an existing open porch, or adding Eze-breeze or similar windows to a porch (Raleigh's permit directory lists building or changing a screened porch as a permit project)
  • Enclosing a porch with a roof — Raleigh requires elevations for screened porches and any structure with a roof
  • Converting a porch into a sunroom (a separate project with its own permit — see the FAQ below)

Usually does NOT need a building permit:

  • Nothing, for building a new screened porch — no jurisdiction publishes an exemption for one.

Rescreening is the gray area. Replacing screen mesh on an existing porch, with no structural change, is non-structural cosmetic work — and the statewide rule exempts non-structural work under $40,000 that does not touch plumbing, mechanical, or electrical systems. But no jurisdiction's pages state a rescreening exemption outright, so do not assume one: confirm with your jurisdiction's office before you start. When in doubt, ask the jurisdiction before you start; that question is free and the wrong guess is not.

Raleigh (inside Raleigh city limits)

Who handles it: The City of Raleigh Planning and Development Department, through its Permit and Development Portal. Register for an account, provide contractor details, or complete an Owner Exemption Affidavit if you are acting as your own contractor. Raleigh's permit directory lists “Decks and Porches — Build or change a deck, porch, screened porch, Eze-breeze windows, or balcony” as a portal project.

What to submit: A residential permit application with a plan set, site plan, and structural elevations. Elevations are required for screened porches and for any structure with a roof, including patios and covered decks.

Extra triggers to know about:

  • Impervious surface: 800 or more square feet of new impervious surface requires a Lot Grading Plan.
  • Septic and wells: A screened porch cannot be built over a septic or wastewater system or its repair area. Properties on well or septic need Wake County Environmental Services approval before applying.
  • Historic districts: A Certificate of Appropriateness from the Raleigh Historic Development Commission is required before you submit the permit application.

Inspections: Raleigh states that when inspections are required, city inspectors check the work at key stages before the project is completed. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Cary (inside Cary town limits)

Who handles it: The Town of Cary Inspections & Permits Department (Cary Town Hall, 316 North Academy Street; 311 or (919) 469-4000). Apply online through the Town's electronic plan review portal; you will need an account for a first application. A homeowner may do their own work if the property will be their primary residence for at least 12 months after completion.

The rule, stated plainly: “Screened or open porch” is on the permit-required list in Cary's Residential Permits & Inspections FAQ. No size or height language accompanies it — a screened porch needs a building permit.

What to submit: A footprint change requires a plot plan and as-built survey. A plot plan and as-built survey are required whenever a footprint changes — which includes a new porch — and an as-built survey is required if the proposed construction is within 5 feet of a setback, easement, or buffer.

Review timing: Porches under 500 square feet are eligible for Cary's SPOT (Small Project Expedited) review.

Inspections: Your required list is determined by permitting staff before issuance and shown on your permit documents. Schedule inspections through Building Permits Online (24 hours a day, 7 days a week), in person at Town Hall (8:30 a.m.–5 p.m., Monday–Friday), or by calling 311 (or (919) 469-4000 from outside Cary).

Wake County (unincorporated areas)

Who handles it — and how to tell: Wake County Inspections & Permits provides building inspections and permitting for all unincorporated areas of Wake County, and, through contractual agreements, inspection services for Knightdale, Rolesville, Wendell, and Zebulon. If you live inside one of those four towns, you apply through the town and Wake County provides the inspections. If you are unsure of your jurisdiction, look your property up on Wake County's iMaps site: if “WC” is listed as your planning jurisdiction, you are in Wake County's jurisdiction. Raleigh, Cary, Apex, Wake Forest, and other municipalities run their own departments and are not covered here.

What to submit (Wake County Permit Portal):

  1. A completed application using the Wake County Permit Portal.
  2. A site plan meeting the County's site plan requirements (entire lot drawn to scale, lot lines and dimensions, existing and proposed structures with dimensions and distances to property lines, driveways, wells/septic or sewer and water easements, water features and flood hazard areas, impervious surfaces itemized in square feet and as a percentage of net lot size, parcel ID, address, north arrow, and scale).
  3. Building plans meeting the County's building plan requirements, drawn to the 2018 North Carolina Residential Code Appendix M. The County's “Build a Deck or Porch” page has a dedicated Porches plan section covering post and header sizes, ceiling joists, roof rafters, and ridge details.
  4. A certificate of workers' compensation insurance, or a completed affidavit of workers' compensation exemption, for contractors.
  5. A notarized affidavit owner-contractor form for projects costing $40,000 or more where an owner is listed as the general contractor.

One wording note: Wake County's page says “porch”, not “screened porch” specifically. A screened porch is a porch with screening, and the page's porch plan requirements — roof rafters, ridge, ceiling joists — plainly cover a roofed screened porch. This is the same page the deck guide cites, and Wake uses one shared “Build a Deck or Porch” page for both.

Extra reviews that may apply:

  • Watershed management: If there are flood hazard areas or your proposed impervious area exceeds the allowable limit, a review is required.
  • Septic/well: If the property is served by an onsite wastewater system, the new structure's location must be checked so it does not impact the system and maintains required setbacks from the wastewater system, its repair area, and any onsite wells. A field consultation fee and site visit by staff may be required. Contacts: Wastewater/Septic 919-856-7434, Groundwater/Well 919-856-7482.

Inspections: Schedule through the Wake County Permit Portal. To get a next-business-day inspection, submit the request before 3:00 p.m. on the previous business day. Your permit documents will list the required inspections.

Licensing notes: A North Carolina licensed general contractor must perform work where the construction cost is $40,000 or more; a property owner may act as their own general contractor if they own the property and will personally occupy it for 12 months after completion.

What happens if you build without one

Raleigh's own residential permits page states the consequences plainly, and they are the right mental model for all three jurisdictions:

  • A citation from the city
  • Fines, increased permit fees, or possible court costs
  • Safety hazards in your home or on your property
  • Having to remove or redo work at the property owner's expense
  • Delays or complications when selling your home or property if unpermitted work is discovered
  • Insurance coverage issues related to unpermitted work

The cheapest version of this story is always the same: get the permit first, while the framing is still visible and the fix is still cheap.

HOA approval is separate from a permit

A city or county permit and your homeowners association's approval are two different things. A permit does not replace HOA architectural review, and HOA approval does not replace a permit — if your neighborhood has an HOA, expect to need both, and check your HOA's documents early, since HOA review runs on its own timeline.

Verify before you build

Permit rules change, and the details that matter — setbacks, floodplain and watershed reviews, septic clearances, historic-district approvals — are specific to your lot. Before you build, confirm your jurisdiction (Cary, Raleigh, or Wake County — see the iMaps check above for unincorporated Wake) and confirm the current requirements with that jurisdiction's office. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a permit for a small screened porch?

Yes. Raleigh requires a building permit when installing a screened porch, Cary lists a screened or open porch on its permit-required list, and Wake County requires an application, site plan, and building plans for a new porch. None of the three jurisdictions states a small-porch exemption, so plan on a permit for any new screened porch, whatever its size.

Do I need a permit to rescreen my existing porch?

No jurisdiction's pages state a rescreening exemption outright. Replacing screen mesh with no structural change is non-structural cosmetic work, and the statewide rule exempts non-structural work under $40,000 that does not touch plumbing, mechanical, or electrical systems — but because no official page says so for rescreening specifically, confirm with your jurisdiction's office before assuming no permit is needed.

Do I need a permit to convert my open porch into a screened porch?

Yes. Raleigh's permit directory lists building or changing a screened porch as a permit project, and screening in a porch makes it a roofed structure — Raleigh requires elevations for screened porches and any structure with a roof. Cary and Wake County treat the conversion the same way as a new porch: a building permit with plans.

Do I need a permit to convert my porch into a sunroom?

Yes. Raleigh lists sunrooms as an addition, and Cary's permit-required list names converting a porch to a sunroom (adding glass or vinyl windows). In Wake County a sunroom is treated as an addition that increases the footprint, so it needs an addition permit with a full site plan and building plans. A sunroom is a separate project from a screened porch, with its own permit.

Can I do the work myself, or does a contractor have to pull the permit?

A homeowner may act as their own general contractor in Cary and in Wake County if they own the property and will personally occupy it for 12 months after completion (in Wake County, a notarized owner-contractor affidavit is required for projects costing $40,000 or more where the owner is the general contractor). In North Carolina, a licensed general contractor must perform work costing $40,000 or more. Raleigh's portal accepts either contractor details or an Owner Exemption Affidavit.

What inspections will my screened porch need?

Your required list is determined by permitting staff before issuance and shown on your permit documents. Raleigh and Wake County confirm inspections at key stages and scheduling through their portals; Cary schedules inspections through Building Permits Online, in person at Town Hall, or by calling 311. Do not assume the inspection list for a deck carries over — a roofed porch has its own requirements, including framing and any electrical work.

What if my property has a septic system or is in a historic district?

Expect extra approvals. In Raleigh, a screened porch cannot be built over a septic or wastewater system or its repair area, and properties on well or septic need Wake County Environmental Services approval before applying; historic homes need a Certificate of Appropriateness before the permit application is submitted. Wake County requires a location check against the septic system, its repair area, and any wells, with setbacks maintained.
